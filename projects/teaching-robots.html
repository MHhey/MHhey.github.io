Robotic arms in a construction planning classroom

Built and programmed 20 open-source robotic arms and ran a hands-on task in a construction planning course, then fed what students measured on the physical task into a scheduling simulation, so the estimate came from a machine they had actually run.

RoleTeaching assistant: build, programming, instruction
CourseProcess Planning and Design · 89 students
StackLeRobot SO-101 · Raspberry Pi 5 · FlexSim
WhenSpring 2026

Overview

Construction students hear a great deal about automation and see very little of it. Site robotics gets discussed as something arriving soon, in the abstract, in a lecture. The gap between that and a machine on a bench you drive yourself is most of the learning, and it is also where an estimate stops being a guess.

For Process Planning and Design I built a fleet of 20 LeRobot SO-101 arms, an open-source robotics platform, each driven by a Raspberry Pi 5. Students worked in groups with the arms directly, on a task with a real time cost, and used that time cost as an input to a scheduling exercise rather than an assumption pulled from a textbook.

What was involved

  1. Build. Assembled and calibrated 20 SO-101 arms from the open-source hardware design — servo setup, wiring, range checks — and set up a Raspberry Pi 5 as the controller for each.
  2. Task. Students were given a project brief and asked to estimate and schedule it. The physical component was a pick-and-place task: lifting plastic bricks and placing them at a specified location with the arm.
  3. Simulation. Students carried what they measured running the task into FlexSim, building a scheduling simulation around it instead of estimating cycle time from assumption.
  4. Classroom. Ran the station across sessions for 89 students, briefed each group, supervised handling, and reset the hardware between groups.

Why it belongs in a planning course

Estimating and scheduling both depend on a task duration somebody has to supply. Usually that number comes from experience, a reference table, or a guess presented with more confidence than it deserves. Running the task on hardware first replaces that guess with a measured number, then lets students see how sensitive their FlexSim schedule is to it.

Notes

  • The hardware is deliberately inexpensive and open-source, which makes the exercise repeatable for a department without a robotics budget.
  • Built and ran this component of the course solo, as TA, including the full arm fleet and controller setup.
  • Sessions were supervised throughout, with the working envelope kept clear during operation.
