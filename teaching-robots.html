Robotic arms in a construction planning classroom

Built and programmed a set of open-source robotic arms and brought them into a construction planning course, so students could work a machine with their hands instead of reading about automation in a slide.

RoleTeaching assistant: build, programming, instruction
CourseProcess Planning and Design · 89 students
WhenSpring 2026

Overview

Construction students hear a great deal about automation and see very little of it. Site robotics is discussed as something arriving soon, in a lecture, in the abstract. The gap between that and a machine on a bench you can drive yourself is most of the learning.

For the spring section of Process Planning and Design I assembled a set of LeRobot arms — an open-source, low-cost robotics platform — and brought them into class as a hands-on station. Students drove the arms directly, recorded their own demonstrations, and watched a task get repeated back.

What was involved

  1. Build. Assembled and calibrated the arms from the open-source hardware design, including servo setup and range checks.
  2. Software. Set up the control stack and the teleoperation and record-and-replay workflow so a first-time user could get a result in a few minutes.
  3. Classroom. Ran the station across sessions, briefed students, supervised handling, and reset the hardware between groups.
  4. Framing. Tied the demonstration back to the course material: what a repeated task means for sequencing, cycle time and labour planning on a real project.

Why it belongs in a planning course

Planning a task and automating a task ask the same question: what exactly are the steps, in what order, with what tolerance for variation. Students who had to teach an arm to repeat a motion found the gaps in their own task breakdown quickly, which is a cheaper way to learn it than on a jobsite.

Notes

  • The hardware is deliberately inexpensive and open-source, which makes the exercise repeatable for a department without a robotics budget.
  • Sessions were supervised throughout, with the working envelope kept clear during operation.
